# Persian Graphic Production

## Copy is a design invariant

Keep an approved text block outside the generated image. Preserve spelling, line intent, punctuation, diacritics, zero-width non-joiners, honorifics, and digit choices supplied by the user. Do not silently modernize a quotation or convert Arabic letter forms in sacred text. For ordinary Persian copy, normalize `ی`/`ک` only when appropriate to the brief; retain the original for comparison.

For Quranic text, hadith, poetry, historical slogans, and names, separate these questions:

- Is this the exact text the user approved?
- Does a verifiable primary source support its wording and attribution?
- Does the artwork render those characters faithfully?

A correct-looking inscription is not proof of a correct attribution. If source verification is required and unavailable, preserve the user's supplied wording and state the limitation rather than inventing a reference.

## Lettering and hierarchy

Choose script and letterform treatment for the message, audience, and reference evidence, not because every religious poster supposedly needs the same calligraphic style. Do not label a font as hand calligraphy or identify a named typeface without evidence.

- Define the main phrase's reading order before expressive overlaps. Preserve distinguishing dots, counters, and word boundaries.
- Use meaningful extensions and ligatures; do not stretch raster lettering horizontally or insert random kashida to fill a box.
- Check joins, baseline behavior, detached marks, dot counts, diacritic collision, and outlines at close view. Then test the entire phrase at final viewing size.
- Keep supporting Persian text in a compatible but clearly subordinate system. A small text block needs its own size, line length, spacing, and contrast, not just a scaled-down display treatment.
- Check RTL paragraphs independently from embedded URLs, dates, Latin acronyms, and mixed-direction numeric sequences. Do not reverse strings manually to mimic shaping.
- Put organizer/credit information where it remains legible without competing with the main phrase. Do not fabricate an organizer or artist credit.

If the user asks for custom calligraphy and no adequate lettering tool or source is available, distinguish a concept proof from commissioned, fully resolved calligraphy. Do not disguise damaged generated words as intentional abstraction.

## Separate artwork from exact typography when needed

Use this production route when exact writing and later editing matter:

1. Lock the copy, hierarchy, and required crop/safe areas.
2. Generate, paint, photograph, or compose the pictorial field with deliberate text space. Do not add fake placeholder inscriptions that could survive unnoticed.
3. Compose verified text using a shaping-capable typography workflow or authorized calligraphic asset. Keep editable text where the tool supports it; preserve an editable master before creating outlined lettering.
4. Integrate lettering using coherent perspective, light, texture, occlusion, and edge treatment without compromising the agreed wording.
5. Inspect the flattened proof against the approved text. An intact editable layer does not prove its exported rendering is correct.

Do not use code-rendered typography as a pretext to replace a requested illustration with a basic programmatic placeholder. The method must serve the visual ambition and output format.

## Material and compositing quality

Choose image scale and detail for the destination. In composites, reconcile light direction, contrast, color cast, perspective, sharpness, grain, contact shadows, and edge halos. Distressed texture should add hierarchy or material credibility, not conceal weak composition or broken lettering.

Review ornaments and repeated motifs for accidental tangencies, broken repetition, compression damage, and inconsistent detail. Keep the intended stylistic irregularity while fixing unintentional artifacts. Do not assert religious/historical authenticity from atmosphere alone; verify any specific depiction that the brief requires to be accurate.

## Output truthfulness

**Digital:** use the requested pixel dimensions and aspect ratio. Check platform crops and safe areas against the intended placement, not a universal guessed margin. Export with an appropriate embedded profile and verify transparency where required. Reopen the exported file rather than trusting the editor preview.

**Print:** obtain final trim size and printer requirements where material. Bleed, safe area, CMYK profile, rich black, spot color, overprint, and PDF standard depend on the production route. Do not prescribe one unsupported setting to every printer. Check effective raster resolution at final size; increasing DPI metadata does not create detail. If no press specification is available, provide a clearly labeled print candidate rather than an unqualified print-ready claim.

**Editable master:** list only what is actually editable. An SVG containing a flattened raster image is not equivalent to editable vector lettering; a renamed image is not a PSD. Respect font and asset licenses, and do not package paid fonts or third-party originals without the necessary permission.

**Variants:** recompose for a materially different ratio. Check hierarchy, image crop, readable text, and logo placement in every requested variant. A mechanically cropped square poster does not automatically work as a vertical story or a wide banner.

## Final inspection

Compare the actual export with the approved brief and text block:

- Full-frame concept and hierarchy; thumbnail recognition; final-size readability.
- Character-by-character main text, diacritics, supporting copy, names, numbers, and dates.
- Edge/crop safety; no accidental text truncation or illegitimate signature.
- Detail artifacts, composites, textures, and controlled consistency across variants.
- Real dimensions, file type, alpha, color profile, and editable-layer claims.

Record any limitation that could not be checked. Deliver the preview, final files, and honest format description; do not claim approval by the referenced artist or that the artist created the output.
